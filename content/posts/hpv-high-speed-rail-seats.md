A chart labeled "Statistics on Sources of HPV in the First Half of 2026" has turned high-speed rail seats into a major source of HPV infection. Superimposed on the image in red text: "High-speed rail seats have been quietly stabbing me in the back."

CCTV reporters set out to trace where the chart came from, and turned up two things: the numbers contradict one another, and many of the accounts posting it are selling HPV disinfectant sprays.

## 1. First, the chart itself

Several similar versions have been circulating, mostly posted between June and August. Most use academic lectures or conference venues as a backdrop, with charts titled "Statistics on Sources of HPV." The scenarios shown are much the same: high-speed rail seats and tearing open packages with bare hands, compared side by side.

After checking, the reporters found the problems lie at the most basic level. Such charts have no clear data source and no clearly defined metrics. For the same statistical item, the figures do not match from one version to the next, and their authenticity cannot be verified.

Among the accounts posting these images are quite a few medical device vendors. Many posts carry hashtags such as "#HPV disinfectant spray#," with products heavily promoted in the comments.

## 2. "Detected" and "infectious" are two different things

Li Tongzeng, chief physician in the infectious disease department at Beijing Youan Hospital, Capital Medical University, told the People's Daily Health Client: it is possible to detect HPV nucleic acid on surfaces such as hotel toilet seats, but detecting nucleic acid does not mean there is live virus, intact virus, or anything infectious.

By his account, even if the chart's data were real, it would at most show that nucleic acid fragments were detected, not live virus. No official body has ever reported a case of HPV infection from a public seat, and the idea does not hold up theoretically either.

He also noted that seats and tray tables on public transport can indeed harbor large amounts of bacteria and even fungi. HPV is relatively fragile in the environment and survives only briefly on dry surfaces.

## 3. Even with live virus, it still needs a way in

For HPV infection to occur, there must first be enough HPV with infectious activity present.

Jiang Lan, associate chief physician in the dermatology department at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University, said HPV prefers warm, humid environments. High-speed rail seats are generally leather or fabric, essentially dry and well ventilated, so large amounts of HPV cannot survive there.

Even if virus did land on you, there is a second barrier. Lun Wenhui, chief physician in the dermatology and venereology department at Beijing Ditan Hospital, Capital Medical University, said cutaneous HPV does spread through skin-to-skin contact, but infection is not easy. Adult skin and mucosa are largely intact, and after contact the virus has difficulty getting inside the skin. There is no need to worry that a train ride will give you common warts, plantar warts, or flat warts—such cases are very rare.

Mucosal HPV types associated with genital tract infection spread mainly through sexual contact; riding a high-speed train will not cause this kind of infection.

Wang Chun, associate chief physician in the dermatology department at Southwest Hospital of Army Medical University, added that infection risk is closely tied to individual condition: people with weaker immunity or underlying diseases (such as diabetes) are more likely to develop symptoms after exposure. Those with a damaged skin barrier, recent shaving or friction, or who sit and sweat for long periods in hot, humid conditions face relatively higher exposure risk. If you fall into these groups, it is all the more advisable to use physical barriers and practice hand hygiene when traveling.

Do disposable seat covers and wiping the tray table with alcohol help? According to Li Tongzeng, these measures are largely psychological. They do help somewhat against bacterial and fungal infections, but have very little effect on reducing HPV risk. If the skin is wounded, cover it with a bandage or sterile gauze.

In CCTV's "Here Comes the Truth," experts advised that for minor skin breaks: keep the area clean day to day and cover it—no need for excessive precautions.

## 4. What actually deserves your attention

In the CCTV report, experts were more specific about what to watch for: rather than worrying about HPV from high-speed rail seats, it is more important not to touch warts on other people's skin directly, and not to share personal items such as clothing worn against the skin.

Li Yi, associate chief physician in the obstetrics and gynecology department at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University, pointed to several specific scenarios: in public bathhouses, try not to sit bare on warm, damp surfaces; contact with someone else's contaminated intimate clothing, or with containers someone else used to wash their genitals, can indeed carry a risk of indirect HPV transmission.
