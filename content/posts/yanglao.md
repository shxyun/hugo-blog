Aging alone means figuring out ahead of time how to use your pension, who will lend a hand if you fall ill, and how to settle your affairs after you're gone. In recent years, more and more elderly people have never married and live alone, and "aging alone" and "retirement planning" have become topics many people search for. Today we won't judge anyone's lifestyle; we'll simply organize "aging as a single person" into a 4-item checklist that ordinary people can follow — pension and cash flow, home care and disability contingency plans, agreed guardianship and wills, and community and aging-friendly resources — explained all at once.

**1. Pension and Cash Flow: First Divide the Money into Three Parts**

The first thing in aging alone is how to arrange your money. The basic pension arrives monthly; divide it into three parts first: **daily expenses** — food, clothing, medicine, water and electricity — on a monthly basis; **emergency reserves** — keep a sum of liquid money that can be withdrawn anytime, useful for medical treatment or hiring help; **supplementary retirement** — that is, the national personal pension scheme.

Regarding the personal pension, the official position is clear: it is a **supplementary pension insurance scheme that is government-supported, individually voluntary, and market-operated**, fully implemented since December 15, 2024; eligibility is for workers who have participated in the basic pension insurance, with a contribution cap of **12,000 yuan per person per year**, and the account operates on a closed basis. As for withdrawals, they can be made upon meeting conditions such as reaching retirement age. Since September 1, 2025, several new circumstances allowing early withdrawal have been added, including suffering a major illness, meeting certain conditions for receiving unemployment insurance benefits, and receiving the minimum living guarantee (dibao).

Here we only discuss the institutional framework. What exactly to buy and whether it's worthwhile varies from person to person — **no products are recommended and no returns are discussed**. When it comes to retirement money, stability matters more than anything.

**2. Home Care and Disability Contingency: Plan for the "What If" in Advance**

Living alone, you need someone to lend a hand most when something unexpected happens. So the second thing is to think about care in advance. While you're still healthy and strong, cooking for yourself and keeping the house tidy is no problem; but as you age, there will come a time when you need help.

In recent years, the state has been promoting a scheme called **long-term care insurance**, abbreviated as LTCI, which covers care for people with disabilities. To be clear: **this scheme is not yet uniformly covered nationwide** — it began as a pilot in 2016 and was expanded in 2020, covering a total of 49 pilot cities. Whether long-term care insurance is available in your city and how to apply for it depends on the local medical insurance authority; don't treat it as insurance that exists everywhere.

**3. Legal Arrangements: Agreed Guardianship and Wills Are Two Different Things**

When spending old age alone, legal matters need to be put in writing in advance. There is one thing here that many people easily confuse.

**One is agreed guardianship.** Article 33 of the Civil Code stipulates: an adult with full capacity for civil conduct may agree in advance with close relatives or other individuals or organizations willing to serve as guardian, **designating their guardian in written form**. When they later lose or partially lose their capacity for civil conduct, this individual or organization shall perform the guardian's duties. In plain terms, while your mind is still clear, you yourself choose someone you trust to make decisions for you in the future.

**The other is a will.** Articles 1134 through 1139 of the Inheritance Book of the Civil Code stipulate six forms of wills — self-written, proxy-written (written by another person on the testator's behalf), printed, audio-video recorded, oral, and notarized — with different witnessing and signature requirements for each. Two points to remember: **first, if several wills exist and their contents conflict, the most recently made one prevails; second, a notarized will no longer has "priority of validity."** A notarized will can also be changed by a will made later.

The most important point: **agreed guardianship does not equal a will.** One governs "who makes decisions for me while I'm alive"; the other governs "how my property is divided after I'm gone." Both need to be done — don't do only one.

**4. Community and Aging-Friendly Resources: They're Right Beside You — Don't Be Shy About Using Them**

The last thing is to make use of the resources around you. Some elderly people feel it's bad to bother others, but in fact these services exist precisely for people like us.

For meals, there is the community's **elderly meal assistance service** — for seniors living alone or with limited mobility, a hot meal is a big deal; many places are also running the **home and community basic elderly care service improvement initiative** (a 2021 project of the Ministry of Civil Affairs and the Ministry of Finance), providing home-visit care, bathing assistance, and cleaning assistance. For **aging-friendly home renovations**, the state has also issued dedicated documents to promote them (Min Fa [2020] No. 86 (民发〔2020〕86号) and Min Fa [2022] No. 9 (民发〔2022〕9号)), and during the "14th Five-Year Plan" period supported 2 million households of seniors in special difficulty with renovations — slip-resistant flooring, grab bars along walls, a small night light for getting up at night, and a shower seat in the bathroom. **How much subsidy is actually available and who can apply varies by region — asking the community is the most accurate.**

In one sentence: **living alone doesn't mean no one is looking after you.** If you know how to use the resources, life becomes much easier.

**The Action Checklist (4 Things)**

1. **Divide the money into three parts**: daily, emergency, and supplementary retirement — know the numbers first;
2. **Plan care in advance**: check your city's long-term care insurance policy and application requirements, and ask the community;
3. **Put legal matters in writing**: arrange agreed guardianship while you're still clear-headed; make the will in a legally recognized form, noting that the most recently made one prevails;
4. **Use the resources**: meal assistance, home-visit services, and aging-friendly renovations — start by asking the community and the civil affairs department.

**A Few Common Misconceptions, Clarified Along the Way**

- **Misconception 1: Living alone means being utterly alone.** There is a safety net in the institutional system and services in the community; the key is whether you're willing to ask and use them.
- **Misconception 2: A notarized will has the highest validity.** Not necessarily. Since the Civil Code, a notarized will no longer takes priority; the most recently made will prevails.
- **Misconception 3: The personal pension is a "high-yield investment."** It is a supplementary pension insurance scheme, not a tool for chasing high returns.
- **Misconception 4: Long-term care insurance is available nationwide.** It is still in the pilot phase and not uniformly covered nationwide; it depends on your local area.

**In Closing**

Aging alone is not about toughing it out in solitude, but about arranging everything you can arrange properly and finding peace of mind. Divide the pension into three parts, plan care in advance, put legal matters in writing, and use the resources around you — these 4 things anyone can follow. Thinking about it early is not about fearing old age; it's about making every day ahead feel more secure.
