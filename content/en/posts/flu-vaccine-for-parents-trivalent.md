Taking your parents for a flu shot comes with a knot in the stomach beforehand. Wasn't there something this year about trivalent versus quadrivalent? What if the nurse asks something you can't answer? Mum has high blood pressure and an egg allergy—can she still get it?

Questions like these have been piling up lately. The reason is that both this year's vaccine composition and the vaccination guidelines have been updated, while advice online has grown muddled. What follows is a family decision guide, in the order you are likely to run into things on the way out the door.

## "Only trivalent this year" is not a downgrade

Start with where the composition comes from. On February 27, 2026, the World Health Organization issued its recommended composition for the 2026–2027 Northern Hemisphere flu vaccine. All three components—A(H1N1), A(H3N2) and B/Victoria—were updated from the previous year. The B/Yamagata line was dropped because no naturally occurring Yamagata virus has been detected anywhere in the world since March 2020.

The Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention published its technical guidelines for 2026–2027 flu vaccination on September 2, 2026. This season it recommends trivalent inactivated vaccine, for anyone aged 6 months and up, and trivalent live attenuated vaccine, for ages 3 to 17.

So what separates trivalent from quadrivalent? Trivalent covers two influenza A strains and one influenza B strain; quadrivalent adds a second B strain. Zhang Zhujiazi, deputy director of the immunization program institute at the Beijing Center for Disease Prevention and Control, puts it this way: under current flu circulation patterns, the real-world protection from trivalent and quadrivalent is basically the same.

None of this makes trivalent a downgrade with weaker protection, and it is not true that quadrivalent has been banned. The guidelines recommend trivalent; what a given clinic actually stocks, and what its doctors advise, depends on the local situation.

## September and October are the sweet spot, not a deadline

After vaccination it usually takes two to four weeks to build protective antibody levels. China generally enters its winter-spring flu season around November, which is why the guidelines suggest finishing shots in September and October. That window is often called the golden period: get the antibodies up before the peak arrives.

One line that frightens people needs correcting. Miss September and October and you can still get vaccinated any time during the season. It still helps. There is no rule that a shot after the end of October is wasted. And if you completed the full course this season already, you don't need another dose.

Doses are easy to mix up too. From age 9 up, it is one dose a year whether or not you have been vaccinated before. Children aged 6 months to 8 years getting inactivated vaccine for the first time need two doses, at least four weeks apart. Your parents are almost certainly in the first group: one shot a year.

## Who should wait, and who can go ahead

With older parents, the sticking point is almost always contraindications. The situations that genuinely rule out vaccination are narrow: allergy to any component of the vaccine—excipients, formaldehyde, splitting agents, antibiotics—or a serious allergic reaction to a flu shot in the past. Beyond that, most conditions are not blanket disqualifiers.

Two worries come up constantly:

- **Egg allergy.** Vaccination is fine with products whose labels do not list egg allergy as a contraindication. The 2025 edition of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia caps ovalbumin in split-virus vaccines at 200 ng per dose. Tell the doctor about the allergy history honestly at the clinic.
- **Pregnancy.** Vaccination is fine with products whose labels do not list pregnancy as a contraindication.

What does call for waiting is a different set of circumstances: a fever, an acute illness, or a chronic condition in an acute flare-up. Get well, or wait until things are stable. Having a chronic disease—high blood pressure, diabetes, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease—actually puts your parents in the priority group. The guidelines recommend vaccination for everyone aged 6 months and up without a contraindication, with priority for key groups including adults 60 and over and people with one or more chronic conditions. When a chronic disease is stable, vaccination is generally fine. Follow the vaccinating doctor's judgment.

Keep expectations reasonable. A flu shot does not guarantee you won't get flu, but it does lower the risk of falling ill and sharply cuts the risk of severe illness and death. Protection usually lasts six to eight months, and the virus mutates every year, which is why the shot is annual. One dose does not cover a year.

## Booking: find the list, then pick the nearest clinic

Flu vaccination is delivered by qualified medical institutions, usually community health service centers and township health centers, with some general hospitals taking part. Local health or CDC authorities publish the list of providers for their area; check the official website, WeChat account or mini-program and book the closest one.

Entry points differ by city. Two examples, for reference only—elsewhere, follow local notices. In Shanghai, you can search and book through Suishenban, Jiankangyun or the Shanghai CDC WeChat account. In Guangzhou, try the Guangzhou CDC iHealth or Guangzhou Vaccination mini-programs.

Call ahead to confirm two things: whether the clinic has vaccine in stock, and its morning and afternoon hours. At the clinic, tell the doctor about allergy history and any medications for chronic conditions. The rest of the process is simpler than you expect.
